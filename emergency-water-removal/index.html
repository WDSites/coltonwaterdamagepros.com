When standing water is an emergency

Any water deep enough to cover the floor, or still flowing, qualifies. A ruptured supply line under a sink can put a few hundred gallons onto a floor in a single night, and a failed water heater can empty a full tank in the time it takes to notice. Water that sits more than a day starts to wick up walls and swell trim.

Call at night too. After-hours calls are common because overflows happen when no one is watching, such as a washer hose that lets go at 2 a.m. or a toilet that runs while the house sleeps. A same-day response, when available, keeps the loss from spreading to the next room.

Colton has some flood exposure of its own. The city's General Plan names the ground beside the Santa Ana River and Reche Canyon as where flooding from rain is most likely, so if your property sits in either area, watch low spots and garages during winter storm season.

Safety first: electricity, sewage and gas

Before you call the restoration line

If water has reached an outlet, a power strip, an appliance or the electrical panel, keep out of the room. The main breaker is worth switching off only when the box sits in a dry spot you can walk to without wading. When it does not, phone the utility or 911 before anything else.

If you smell gas, leave the building and call 911 or the gas company from outside. If the water came from a toilet or sewer line, treat it as contaminated, keep everyone out and call us immediately.

How standing water is removed

Crews arrive with submersible pumps for deep water and truck-mounted or portable extraction units for shallow water and carpet. Wet vacuums handle corners and stairs. The aim is to take out as much liquid as possible before drying equipment is set.

Once the visible water is gone, a moisture meter and a thermal imaging camera show what the surface cannot. Wet pad under carpet is usually removed and discarded, while hard surfaces are extracted and left to dry. Anything that stays damp is handed to the drying stage described on the water mitigation page.

What affects the cost of emergency water removal

Water category

Clean supply-line water costs less to handle than gray water or sewage, which needs protective gear and disinfecting.

Volume and depth

A damp kitchen and a room with several inches of water need different pumps and different labor hours.

Materials affected

Carpet and pad, hardwood, laminate and drywall each respond differently, and some must be removed.

Time since the loss

Water that has sat longer has moved further, so more rooms and materials need drying.

Equipment and visits

Pumps, extraction units and later drying equipment add to the scope, and monitoring visits depend on the building.

Nobody can price a flood from a doorway photo, so the number comes after we know the volume and what got wet. Call and describe the water to start that conversation.

Insurance notes for a sudden water loss

Policies tend to respond when a pipe bursts or an appliance fails without warning, and tend not to respond when a slow leak was ignored. Outside floodwater is excluded from most homeowners policies, so it takes a flood policy. The California Department of Insurance tells homeowners to let their insurer know about water damage right away.

Take photos before moving anything, keep receipts, and ask your insurer what applies to your policy. We cannot promise coverage.

What to do while you wait

  1. Shut off the main water valve. Know where it is before an emergency happens.
  2. Switch off the breaker for wet areas only when the panel is in a dry spot you can walk to.
  3. Lift furniture, rugs, electronics and paperwork off the floor.
  4. Photograph the water, the source and any damaged belongings.
  5. Open windows if weather allows, and keep children and pets away from the water.
