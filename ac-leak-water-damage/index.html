How an AC leak becomes water damage in a Colton home

An air conditioner pulls moisture out of the air as it cools, and that moisture, called condensate, drips into a pan and leaves through a small drain line. When the line clogs with algae and dust, or the pan rusts through, the water goes where the equipment sits: an attic, a closet, a wall cavity or a ceiling.

Colton systems run hard. NOAA climate data for the San Bernardino station, from the 1971 to 2000 normals, lists average daily highs of 96.0 degrees in July and 96.2 in August. A unit that cycles most of the day in those months makes far more condensate than one running a few hours, so a drain line that was marginal in spring can overflow by midsummer.

Equipment location decides the damage. An attic air handler leaks onto insulation and a ceiling, a closet unit leaks into carpet and baseboards, and a ductless wall unit streaks the wall below it. Each pattern needs a different drying plan, and none of them is a roof leak, which has a different source and a different fix.

Why AC leak damage shows up days after it starts

A pan can overflow slowly for weeks above a ceiling. Attic insulation soaks up the water first, the drywall absorbs it from above, and the stain appears only after the board is saturated. By then the wet area is usually larger than the stain.

The delay matters for mold. The state insurance department's residential claims guide observes that water damage pointing to leakage over time may already involve mold. That is one reason a stained ceiling deserves a moisture reading instead of a coat of paint.

When an AC leak needs a call right now

A bulging ceiling, water dripping through a light fixture or a closet floor that stays wet after the unit stops all need a quick call. The line is answered at every hour, including nights and weekends, and any free crew can be out that same day.

A faint stain with no drip is less urgent but still worth a reading. Wet insulation keeps working on the drywall long after the unit is fixed.

First-hour safety steps for an AC leak

An air handler is electrical equipment sitting above or beside water, so the order of steps matters.

  1. Turn the system off at the thermostat, then trip its breaker if the panel sits somewhere dry that you can walk to. A running blower pushes more water out of the pan.
  2. Stay out from under a bulging or dripping ceiling. Water-loaded drywall can fall without warning.
  3. If water drips through a light fixture or you see sparks, step away and ring 911 or the city's electric utility before anyone else.
  4. Do not climb into the attic to look. It is hot, the ceiling below is not a floor, and the wiring may be wet.
  5. Catch drips with a bucket where it is safe, move belongings away, and photograph the stain, the unit and the drain line.
  6. Call an HVAC technician for the unit and call us for the wet building materials.

That guide also warns that homeowners policies may not pay for ensuing damage when reasonable steps were skipped. Shutting the system off and documenting what you see are part of those steps.

What our crew does and where the HVAC technician fits

The HVAC technician clears the drain line, replaces a failed pan, checks the coil and installs or tests a float switch that stops the unit when the pan fills. We do not repair air conditioning equipment. We handle the building materials the water reached.

Crews scan the ceiling and walls using moisture meters plus a thermal camera, then opens saturated drywall or insulation. Wet attic insulation holds water and rarely dries well, so it is often removed. Air movers and LGR dehumidifiers dry out the cavity, and the readings from each visit are compared with the goals in ANSI/IICRC S500, the published water damage reference.

Once the materials read dry, the ceiling can be patched and repainted. That work belongs to our water damage repair service, and the drying stage follows the process on the water mitigation page.

AC overflows and your policy

Sudden overflow versus a long neglected drain

A pan that cracks overnight or a drain that overflows during a heat wave usually reads as sudden in policy language, and many policies respond to the resulting damage. A line left clogged for a long season, or a stain ignored after the first sign, may be treated as gradual or maintenance related. Check the exact policy wording with your insurer.

AC water is not flood water, so a flood policy is not the question. Keep the HVAC technician's invoice, photographs of the pan and drain, and our moisture log so the file shows the cause and the extent.

Prevention: keep the condensate line clear

  • Have the drain line flushed and the pan checked before the hot season starts.
  • Ask the technician to fit a float switch or pan sensor on attic and closet units.
  • Change filters on schedule, because restricted airflow can freeze a coil that later thaws into the pan.
  • Look at the ceiling under the attic unit and the closet floor once a month in summer.
  • On ductless units, keep the drain hose sloped and free of kinks.
